\chapter{Introduction and Background}
\label{ch:introduction}

The \emph{joint spectral radius} (JSR), introduced by \citet{RotaStrang1960NoteJSR}, measures the maximal asymptotic growth of products drawn from a matrix family.
It governs stability under arbitrary switching and appears in approximation theory.
Exact computation is difficult because the number of candidate products grows exponentially with their length.
The invariant-polytope algorithm \citep{GuglielmiProtasov2013ExactComputationJSR} and finite-tree algorithm \citep{MoellerReif2014TreeBasedJSR} address this difficulty with different finite certificates.

We study the invariant-tree algorithm of \citet{MejstrikReif2025HybridApproachJSR} and prove its certificate for separate test trees at different vertices by induction.
Numerical experiments compare ordinary IPA with its hybrid extension.
For one matrix pair, the hybrid run closes a test tree while ordinary IPA reaches the prescribed iteration limit.
The remaining theory follows the cited literature.

\section{Notation and Preliminaries}
\label{sec:introPreliminaries}

Unless stated otherwise, all matrices are real $d \times d$ matrices and all matrix norms are submultiplicative.
Base matrix families are finite and nonempty, with $d,n\geq1$.
The following notation is used throughout:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Matrix families and dimensions:
    \[
        \mathcal{A}=\{A_1,\dots,A_n\},\qquad
        \tilde{\mathcal{A}}=\{\tilde{A}_1,\dots,\tilde{A}_n\},\qquad
        d=\text{matrix dimension},\quad n=|\mathcal{A}|.
    \]
    \item Generator notation:
    \[
        \mathcal{G}=\{G_1,\dots,G_m\},\qquad m=|\mathcal{G}|.
    \]
    \item Positive indices and products:
    \[
        \mathcal{I}:=\{1,\dots,n\},\quad
        I=[i_1,\dots,i_k]\in\mathcal{I}^k,\quad
        A_I:=A_{i_k}\cdots A_{i_1},\quad
        \mathcal{A}^k:=\{A_I:I\in\mathcal{I}^k\}.
    \]
    \item Scalars and spectra: $\alpha$ denotes a scalar, $x$ a vector, and $\rho(\cdot)$ the spectral radius.
    Eigenvalues, candidate scaling factors, and other local constants are defined where they occur.
    \item Polytope norms:
    \[
        \lVert \cdot\rVert _{\mathrm{co}_{\mathrm{s}}(V)}=\lVert \cdot\rVert _V.
    \]
\end{itemize}

Appendix~\ref{app:notation} contains the full notation glossary used throughout the thesis.

\section{Motivation and Applications of the Joint Spectral Radius}
\label{sec:introMotivationJSR}

Let $\mathcal{A} = \{A_1,\dots, A_n\}$ be a finite set of $d\times d$ matrices.
Consider a discrete-time linear system generated by a matrix sequence from $\mathcal{A}$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:switched_system}
    x_{t+1} = B_t x_t, \quad x_0 \in \R^d,
\end{equation}
where $B_t \in \mathcal{A}$ for all $t \in \N_0$.

A key question is whether the system is \emph{uniformly asymptotically stable}: trajectories must converge to zero uniformly over switching sequences and initial states in the unit ball.
In particular, for every fixed initial state and every switching sequence,
\[
    \lim_{t \to \infty} x_t = 0.
\]
This stability question is answered by a generalization of the spectral radius for a single matrix, the so-called \emph{joint spectral radius (JSR)} of a matrix set $\mathcal{A}$ \citep{RotaStrang1960NoteJSR}.
\begin{definition}\citep{RotaStrang1960NoteJSR}
    \label{def:jsrDefinition}
    For a finite set of matrices $\mathcal{A} = \{A_1, \dots, A_n\}$, the JSR is defined as:
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:jsr_def}
        \JSR(\mathcal{A}) := \lim_{k \to \infty} \max_{P \in \mathcal{A}^k}  \lVert P\rVert ^{1/k}.
    \end{equation}
    Here $\lVert \cdot\rVert $ denotes a submultiplicative matrix norm.
    The limit and norm independence are justified in Theorem~\ref{thm:jsrWellDefined}.
\end{definition}

\subsection*{Illustrative Example}

Consider the matrix set:
\[
    \mathcal{A} = \left\{ A_1 =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0.5 & 1 \\
        0 & 0.5
    \end{bmatrix}, \quad
    A_2 =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0.3 & 0 \\
        1 & 0.3
    \end{bmatrix}
    \right\}.
\]
Each matrix individually has spectral radius less than $1$.
However, their product
\[
    A_1 A_2 =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0.5 & 1 \\
        0 & 0.5
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0.3 & 0 \\
        1 & 0.3
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1.15 & 0.3 \\
        0.5 & 0.15
    \end{bmatrix}
\]
has spectral radius of approximately
\[
    \rho(A_1 A_2) \approx 1.2825 > 1.
\]
Repeated copies of $A_1A_2$ grow exponentially along its leading eigenvector.
The lower bound is
\[
    \JSR(\mathcal{A})\geq\rho(A_1A_2)^{1/2}>1,
\]
although the powers of either matrix alone converge to zero.
Individual spectral radii below $1$ do not provide a common contracting norm.

The following results explain the role of the JSR in switched systems \citep{Jungers2009JointSpectralRadius}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $\JSR(\mathcal{A})<1$, every switching trajectory converges to zero uniformly at an exponential rate.
    \item If $\JSR(\mathcal{A})=1$, boundedness requires a separate argument.
    Irreducibility is a sufficient condition for uniformly bounded products, but is not necessary.
\end{itemize}
The JSR also appears in subdivision theory \citep{CavarettaDahmenMicchelli1991StationarySubdivision} and wavelet theory \citep{DaubechiesLagarias1992TwoScaleII,Maesumi2000JSRHolderWavelets}.
Other applications include coding-theoretic capacity \citep{MoisionOrlitskySiegel2001CodesAvoidDifferences,BlondelJungersProtasov2006CapacityCodes} and discrete mathematics \citep{Protasov2000PartitionFunctionAsymptotic,JungersProtasovBlondel2008IntegerMatrixGrowth,JungersProtasovBlondel2009OverlapFreeWordsSpectra}.

\section{Fundamental Theoretical Results}
\label{sec:introTheoreticalBackground}

Fekete's lemma gives the limit needed in the definition of the JSR \citep{Jungers2009JointSpectralRadius}.

\begin{lemma}[Fekete's Lemma]\label{lem:feketeLemma}
    Let $(a_n)_{n\geq 1}$ be a sequence of real numbers such that
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:subadditive}
        a_{m+n} \leq a_m + a_n \qquad \text{for all } m,n\geq 1.
    \end{equation}
    Then the limit
    \[
        \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{n}
    \]
    exists in $\R\cup\{-\infty\}$ and equals $\inf_{n\geq 1}a_n/n$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Set $a_0:=0$ and $\alpha:=\inf_{n\geq1}a_n/n$.
    Fix $n\geq1$.
    For every sufficiently large $k$, write $k=qn+r$ with $q\geq1$ and $0\leq r<n$.
    Subadditivity gives $a_k\leq q a_n+a_r$.
    The finitely many possible remainder terms $a_r$ are bounded, while $q/k$ tends to $1/n$.
    Hence
    \[
        \limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{a_k}{k}\leq\frac{a_n}{n}.
    \]
    Taking the infimum over $n$ yields $\limsup a_k/k\leq\alpha$.
    By definition, $a_k/k\geq\alpha$ for every $k$, so $\liminf a_k/k\geq\alpha$.
    The limit is therefore $\alpha$, including the case $\alpha=-\infty$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\citep{RotaStrang1960NoteJSR,Jungers2009JointSpectralRadius}
    \label{thm:jsrWellDefined}
    The JSR is well-defined and independent of the choice of the matrix norm.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    For a fixed matrix norm, let $M_k:=\max_{P\in\mathcal{A}^k}\lVert P\rVert$.
    Submultiplicativity gives $M_{k+\ell}\leq M_kM_\ell$.
    If some $M_k=0$, all sufficiently long products vanish and the limit defining the JSR is zero.
    Otherwise $\log M_k$ is subadditive, so Lemma~\ref{lem:feketeLemma} shows that $M_k^{1/k}$ has a limit.

    For two matrix norms, finite-dimensional norm equivalence gives positive constants $c,C$ such that $c\lVert P\rVert_1\leq\lVert P\rVert_2\leq C\lVert P\rVert_1$ for every $P\in\R^{d\times d}$.
    The same inequalities hold for the maxima over $\mathcal{A}^k$.
    Taking $k$-th roots and letting $k$ tend to infinity removes the factors $c$ and $C$, so both norms give the same JSR.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Homogeneity of the Joint Spectral Radius}
\begin{proposition}\citep{Jungers2009JointSpectralRadius}
    \label{prop:jsrHomogeneity}
    The JSR is homogeneous, meaning for any set of matrices $\mathcal{A}$ and scalar $\alpha \in \R$ we have
    \begin{equation}
        \JSR(\alpha \mathcal{A}) = |\alpha| \JSR(\mathcal{A}).
    \end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    For every $k\geq1$, scalar multiplication gives
    \[
        \max_{P\in(\alpha\mathcal{A})^k}\lVert P\rVert^{1/k}
        =|\alpha|\max_{P\in\mathcal{A}^k}\lVert P\rVert^{1/k}.
    \]
    Taking limits proves the assertion, including $\alpha=0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Berger--Wang Characterization and Three-Member Inequality}
\begin{proposition}\citep{BergerWang1992BoundedSemigroupsMatrices}
    \label{prop:bergerWangCharacterization}
    The $\JSR$ can equivalently be defined as
    \[
        \JSR(\mathcal{A}) = \limsup_{k \to \infty} \max_{P \in \mathcal{A}^k} \rho(P)^{1/k},
    \]
    if $\mathcal{A}$ is a finite (or compact) set.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}\citep{RotaStrang1960NoteJSR,Jungers2009JointSpectralRadius}\label{prop:threeMemberInequality}
    Let $\lVert \cdot\rVert $ be a matrix norm.
    Then for every $k\in\N$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:three_member}
        \max_{P\in\mathcal{A}^k}\rho(P)^{1/k}\ \leq\ \JSR(\mathcal{A})\ \leq\ \max_{P\in\mathcal{A}^k}\lVert P\rVert ^{1/k}.
    \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Fix $k$ and set $M_j:=\max_{Q\in\mathcal{A}^j}\lVert Q\rVert$.
    Since $M_{j+\ell}\leq M_jM_\ell$, the submultiplicative limit in Theorem~\ref{thm:jsrWellDefined} equals $\inf_{j\geq1}M_j^{1/j}$.
    In particular, $\JSR(\mathcal{A})\leq M_k^{1/k}$.

    For any $P\in\mathcal{A}^k$, its $r$-th power lies in $\mathcal{A}^{kr}$ and $\rho(P)^r=\rho(P^r)\leq\lVert P^r\rVert\leq M_{kr}$.
    Taking $(kr)$-th roots and letting $r$ tend to infinity gives $\rho(P)^{1/k}\leq\JSR(\mathcal{A})$.
    Maximizing over $P$ proves the other inequality.
\end{proof}

This inequality underlies many computational methods, since it yields computable lower and upper bounds on $\JSR(\mathcal{A})$.
As $k\to\infty$, the upper bound $\max_{P\in\mathcal{A}^k}\lVert P\rVert ^{1/k}$ converges to $\JSR(\mathcal{A})$.
The lower bounds $\max_{P\in\mathcal{A}^k}\rho(P)^{1/k}$ need not converge, but their limit superior equals $\JSR(\mathcal{A})$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:bergerWangCharacterization}.

\subsection*{Similarity Invariance and Reducibility}

\begin{proposition}\citep{Jungers2009JointSpectralRadius}
    \label{prop:jsrSimilarityInvariance}
    The $\JSR$ is invariant under similarity transformations, i.e., for any invertible matrix $T$ we have
    \begin{equation}
        \JSR(\mathcal{A}) = \JSR(T^{-1}\mathcal{A}T).
    \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    For every word $I\in\mathcal{I}^k$, the transformed product is $T^{-1}A_IT$.
    Similarity preserves its spectral radius, so the maxima in the Berger--Wang characterization agree at every length $k$.
    Taking the limit superior proves the assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\citep{Jungers2009JointSpectralRadius}
    \label{def:matrixSetReducibility}
    A set of matrices is called \emph{(commonly) reducible} if there exists a nontrivial subspace of $\R^d$ that is invariant under all matrices in the set.
    This means there exists a change of basis that block-triangularizes all matrices in $\mathcal{A}$ at the same time.
    If $\mathcal{A}$ is not reducible, it is called \emph{irreducible}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\citep{Protasov1997GeneralizedSpectralRadiusGeometric}
    \label{prop:reducibleBlockDecomposition}
    If $\mathcal{A}$ is reducible, then
    \begin{equation}
        \JSR(\mathcal{A}) = \max \{ \JSR(\mathcal{B}), \JSR(\mathcal{D}) \},
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathcal{B} = \{B_1,\dots, B_n\}$ and $\mathcal{D} = \{D_1,\dots, D_n\}$ are the blocks of the block-triangularized matrices
    \[
        T^{-1}A_jT =
        \begin{bmatrix}
            B_j & C_j \\
            0   & D_j
        \end{bmatrix}
    \]
    under some invertible matrix $T$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{example}
    \label{ex:reducibleMatricesBlockForm}
    \[
        A_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
        A_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
    \]

    The common eigenvectors exhibit a common invariant line:

    \[
        v_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
        v_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}.
    \]

    Using the change of basis matrix

    \[
        T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix},
    \]

    we compute the similarity transformations

    \[
        T^{-1} A_1 T = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
        T^{-1} A_2 T = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
    \]

    Since both matrices are upper triangular in this basis, they share a common invariant subspace, proving that they are reducible.
    Now, from Proposition~\ref{prop:reducibleBlockDecomposition}, we know that the $\JSR$ can be calculated as
    \[
        \JSR(\mathcal{A}) = \max \{ \JSR(\{3,5\}), \JSR(\{1,1\})\} = 5
    \]
\end{example}

This can be applied iteratively until the sets of blocks are all irreducible.
The problem is thereby split into similar problems of smaller dimension.
Irreducibility will be imposed only when required by a cited existence or termination theorem.
The finite-tree and hybrid upper-bound certificates also apply to reducible families.

\subsection*{Finiteness Property and Spectrum-Maximizing Products}
\begin{definition}\citep{LagariasWang1995FinitenessConjecture}
    \label{def:finitenessProperty}
    A matrix set $\mathcal{A} = \{ A_1, \dots, A_n \}$ is said to possess the \emph{finiteness property} if there exists a finite sequence of matrices $A_{i_1}, \dots, A_{i_k}$ such that
    \begin{equation}
        \JSR(\mathcal{A}) = \rho(A_{i_k} \cdots A_{i_1})^{1/k}.
    \end{equation}
    Any such product is referred to as a \emph{spectrum-maximizing product} (s.m.p.).
\end{definition}
The finiteness property does not hold for every matrix family \citep{BlondelTheysVladimirov2003CounterexampleFiniteness}.
An exact certificate matching a finite candidate product necessarily verifies this property for the family under consideration.

\subsection*{Computational Complexity of JSR Computation}

Approximating the JSR is NP-hard in general \citep{TsitsiklisBlondel1997HardToComputeJSR}.
Thus one does not expect a polynomial-time algorithm unless $\text{P}=\text{NP}$.
For rational matrix families, deciding whether the JSR is at most one is undecidable \citep{BlondelTsitsiklis2000BoundednessUndecidable}.
Structured instances can still admit efficient numerical treatment.

\subsection*{S.m.p.-Candidates and Generators}
The algorithms studied here begin by searching for products with large averaged spectral radius.
These products provide lower bounds on the JSR and candidates for the subsequent verification step.
After normalization, selected products satisfying the spectral bound can also serve as generators in the tree algorithms.

\subsection*{Spectral Gap}
\begin{definition}\citep[Definition 1.9]{MejstrikReif2025HybridApproachJSR}
    \label{def:spectralGap}
    A finite matrix family $\mathcal{A}$ is said to possess a \emph{spectral gap} if there exists a constant $\gamma \in [0,1)$ such that for every $k \in \N$ and every product $A_I \in \mathcal{A}^k$,
    \[
        \rho(A_I) = \JSR(\mathcal{A})^k
        \quad \text{or} \quad
        \rho(A_I) \leq \gamma\,\JSR(\mathcal{A})^k.
    \]
    Equivalently, every non-s.m.p.\ product is uniformly separated from the maximal growth rate by the factor $\gamma < 1$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    \label{rem:spectralGapHybrid}
    The strict IPA closure characterization requires a spectral gap, uniqueness of the s.m.p.\ candidate up to cyclic permutations and powers, and a simple leading eigenvalue \citep{MejstrikReif2025HybridApproachJSR}.
    Later chapters show how these conditions relate to the algorithms.
    \citet{GuglielmiProtasov2024SpectralGapFamilyMatrices} study a different notion of gap, between the first and second Lyapunov exponents for matrix families.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
    \label{ex:spectralGap}
    Consider
    \[
        A_1=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix},
        \qquad
        A_2=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix},
        \qquad
        \mathcal{A}=\{A_1,A_2\}.
    \]
    Since $\max\{\rho(A_1),\rho(A_2)\}=2$ and $\lVert A_1\rVert _\infty=\lVert A_2\rVert _\infty=2$, we get $\JSR(\mathcal{A})=2$.
    If the word $I$ contains $r$ occurrences of the first index, its product has the form
    \[
        A_I = A_1^r A_2^{k-r}
        = \begin{bmatrix}2^r&0\\0&2^{k-r}\end{bmatrix},
    \]
    so
    \[
        \rho(A_I)=2^{\max\{r,k-r\}}.
    \]
    If $r=0$ or $r=k$, then $\rho(A_I)=2^k=\JSR(\mathcal{A})^k$ and $A_I$ is an s.m.p.\ product.
    If $1\leq r\leq k-1$, then
    \[
        \rho(A_I)\leq 2^{k-1}=\tfrac12\,\JSR(\mathcal{A})^k.
    \]
    Hence Definition~\ref{def:spectralGap} holds with $\gamma=\tfrac12$.
\end{example}

\section{Preprocessing and Normalization}
\label{sec:introPreprocessing}
The algorithms seek to certify a candidate value for the finite family $\mathcal{A}$.
They share the following preprocessing steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reduction}: If desired, split a reducible family into smaller diagonal-block problems.
    This is not a prerequisite for the upper-bound certificates proved below.
    \item \textbf{Candidate}: Select a nonempty product $P=A_{i_k}\cdots A_{i_1}$ with positive averaged spectral radius $\lambda=\rho(P)^{1/k}>0$.
    A zero-radius candidate cannot supply this scaling and requires a different candidate or a separate analysis.
    \item \textbf{Rescaling}: Set $\tilde{A}_i=\lambda^{-1}A_i$.
    The corresponding product is $\tilde{P}=\lambda^{-k}P$, with $\rho(\tilde{P})=1$.
    \item \textbf{Verification}: The candidate gives $\JSR(\tilde{\mathcal{A}})\geq1$.
    Prove the reverse inequality using a polytope or tree certificate.
    Homogeneity then yields $\JSR(\mathcal{A})=\lambda$.
\end{enumerate}

Scaling a candidate does not by itself imply $\rho(\tilde{A}_i)\leq1$ for every base matrix.
That property holds only if $\lambda\geq\max_i\rho(A_i)$.
If it fails, the candidate is already too small to be spectrum maximizing.
The node-growth lemma in Chapter~\ref{ch:finiteTree} does not require this extra base-matrix assumption.
Every selected generator, however, must independently satisfy its stated spectral-radius bound.

The algorithms differ in the certificate used for the upper inequality.
IPA constructs a polytope invariant under every scaled matrix.
The finite-tree method bounds uncovered leaf families and controls an internal remainder polynomially.
The hybrid method uses tree tests at individual vertices of an evolving polytope.
