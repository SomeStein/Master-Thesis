\chapter{Invariant-Polytope Algorithm}
\label{ch:invariantPolytope}

The invariant-polytope algorithm of \citet{GuglielmiProtasov2013ExactComputationJSR} seeks a polytope whose norm certifies the candidate JSR value.
Chapters~\ref{ch:finiteTree} and~\ref{ch:hybridApproach} replace invariance under each matrix by conditions on tree leaves.

\section{Overview}

After the preprocessing in Section~\ref{sec:introPreprocessing}, a candidate product supplies the lower bound $\JSR(\tilde{\mathcal{A}})\geq1$.
The algorithm seeks a balanced polytope $\mathcal{P}$ invariant under every matrix in $\tilde{\mathcal{A}}$.
Its Minkowski functional then gives a norm in which every normalized matrix has operator norm at most $1$.
This proves the matching upper bound.
The resulting norm is extremal for the original matrix family once the candidate value is certified.

\section{Algorithmic Formulation}
\label{sec:invAlgorithmStructure}

The initial finite set $V_0=\{v_1,\dots,v_M\}$ must span $\R^d$.
The spanning assumption makes $\mathrm{co}_{\mathrm{s}}(V_0)$ a norm unit ball.
The algorithm tests images $\tilde{A}v$ of pending vertices and appends those outside the current polytope.
Images already inside remain inside when the polytope grows, so they need not be tested again.
When the queue is empty, every stored vertex image lies in the final polytope.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
    \caption{Invariant-polytope algorithm}
    \label{alg:invariantPolytope}
    \begin{algorithmic}
        \State \Input{Normalized family $\tilde{\mathcal{A}}$ and finite spanning set $V_0$}
        \State $V\gets V_0$
        \State $Q\gets V_0$
        \While{$Q\neq\emptyset$}
            \State Remove a vertex $v$ from $Q$
            \For{$\tilde{A}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}$}
                \If{$\lVert\tilde{A}v\rVert_V>1$}
                    \State $u\gets\tilde{A}v$
                    \State $V\xleftarrow{\cup}\{u\}$
                    \State $Q\xleftarrow{\cup}\{u\}$
                \EndIf
            \EndFor
        \EndWhile
        \State \Return $\mathrm{co}_{\mathrm{s}}(V)$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Termination Theory}
\label{sec:invExtremalNorms}

\subsection{Invariant and Extremal Norms}

\begin{definition}\citep{Barabanov1988LyapunovIndicatorDiscreteInclusions}
    \label{def:barabanovNorm}
    For a finite matrix family $\mathcal{A}$, a vector norm is \emph{invariant}, or a \emph{Barabanov norm}, if a scalar $\lambda\geq0$ satisfies
    \[
        \max_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\lVert Ax\rVert=\lambda\lVert x\rVert
        \quad\text{for every }x\in\R^d.
    \]
\end{definition}

The scalar $\lambda$ equals $\JSR(\mathcal{A})$.
Indeed, successive maximizers give $\max_{P\in\mathcal{A}^k}\lVert Px\rVert=\lambda^k\lVert x\rVert$.
Taking the supremum over the unit sphere gives the same equality for the induced operator norms and then the claimed JSR value.

\begin{theorem}\citep{Barabanov1988LyapunovIndicatorDiscreteInclusions}
    \label{thm:barabanovNormExistence}
    Every finite irreducible real matrix family possesses a Barabanov norm.
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}
    \label{ex:barabanovExtremalNorm}
    Consider the rotation and reflection
    \[
        A_1=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad
        A_2=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
        \mathcal{A}=\{A_1,A_2\}.
    \]
    Both preserve Euclidean length, so $\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\lVert Ax\rVert_2=\lVert x\rVert_2$.
    Thus the Euclidean norm is a Barabanov norm and $\JSR(\mathcal{A})=1$.
    We will also construct a polytope unit ball for this family.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}\citep{Protasov1996JSRInvariantSets}
    \label{def:extremalNorm}
    A vector norm is \emph{extremal} for $\mathcal{A}$ if
    \[
        \max_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\lVert Ax\rVert
        \leq\JSR(\mathcal{A})\lVert x\rVert
        \quad\text{for every }x\in\R^d.
    \]
\end{definition}

A Barabanov norm is extremal.
The upper-bound certificate below also applies to reducible families.

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:extremalNormProductGrowth}
    If a vector norm is extremal for $\mathcal{A}$, its induced matrix norm satisfies
    \[
        \max_{P\in\mathcal{A}^k}\lVert P\rVert=\JSR(\mathcal{A})^k
        \quad\text{for every }k\in\N.
    \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Extremality gives $\lVert A_i\rVert\leq\JSR(\mathcal{A})$ for every $i$.
    Submultiplicativity bounds every length-$k$ product by $\JSR(\mathcal{A})^k$.
    The reverse inequality follows by raising the upper inequality in \eqref{eq:three_member} to the power $k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    \label{rem:extremalNormExactComputation}
    An explicitly known extremal norm reduces the JSR to $\max_i\lVert A_i\rVert$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Polytopes as Norm Unit Balls}

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:balancedPolytope}
    For a finite spanning set $V=\{v_1,\dots,v_M\}\subset\R^d$, its \emph{balanced polytope} is
    \[
        \mathcal{P}=\mathrm{co}_{\mathrm{s}}(V)=\mathrm{co}(V\cup(-V))
        =\left\{\sum_{i=1}^M\alpha_iv_i:\sum_{i=1}^M|\alpha_i|\leq1\right\}.
    \]
    It is \emph{invariant under $\mathcal{A}$} if $A\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}$ for every $A\in\mathcal{A}$.
\end{definition}

The spanning condition puts the origin in the interior of $\mathcal{P}$.
The polytope is also bounded, closed, convex, and symmetric.
These properties make its gauge a norm.

\begin{definition}\citep{MagarilIlyaevTikhomirov2001ConvexAnalysis}
    \label{def:minkowskiFunctional}
    For a convex set $C\subseteq\R^d$ with $0\in\operatorname{int}(C)$, its \emph{Minkowski functional} is
    \[
        p_C(x)=\inf\{t>0:x\in tC\},\qquad x\in\R^d.
    \]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    \label{rem:balancedPolytopeInducedNorm}
    A general convex gauge need not be symmetric or positive definite.
    For the balanced polytope $\mathcal{P}$ above, the gauge is a vector norm with unit ball exactly $\mathcal{P}$.
    We write it as $\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}$, or $\lVert\cdot\rVert_V$.
    The same notation denotes its induced operator norm when the argument is a matrix.
\end{remark}

It suffices to test invariance at the stored vertices.
Indeed, if $Av_i\in\mathcal{P}$ for every $i$ and $x=\sum_i\alpha_iv_i$ with $\sum_i|\alpha_i|\leq1$, then
\[
    \lVert Ax\rVert_V
    \leq\sum_i|\alpha_i|\,\lVert Av_i\rVert_V\leq1.
\]
Hence $A\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}$ and $\lVert A\rVert_V\leq1$.

\subsection{Upper-Bound Certificate}

\begin{theorem}\citep{GuglielmiProtasov2013ExactComputationJSR}
    \label{thm:invariantPolytopeImpliesJsrUpperBound}
    Let $\hat\rho=\rho(P)^{1/k}>0$ for a candidate $P\in\mathcal{A}^k$, and set $\tilde{\mathcal{A}}=\hat\rho^{-1}\mathcal{A}$.
    If a balanced polytope $\mathcal{P}$ is invariant under $\tilde{\mathcal{A}}$, then $\JSR(\mathcal{A})=\hat\rho$.
    Its Minkowski functional is an extremal norm for $\mathcal{A}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Invariance gives $\lVert\tilde{A}_i\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}\leq1$ and hence $\lVert A_i\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}\leq\hat\rho$ for every $i$.
    Every length-$r$ product therefore has norm at most $\hat\rho^r$, proving $\JSR(\mathcal{A})\leq\hat\rho$.
    The candidate gives $\hat\rho\leq\JSR(\mathcal{A})$ by \eqref{eq:three_member}.
    Equality follows, and $\lVert A_ix\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}\leq\JSR(\mathcal{A})\lVert x\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}$ proves extremality.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A finite invariant-polytope certificate]
    \label{ex:ipaDiamondCertificate}
    Return to the rotation and reflection in Example~\ref{ex:barabanovExtremalNorm}.
    With $V=\{e_1,e_2\}$, the polytope is the diamond
    \[
        \mathcal{P}=\{(x_1,x_2)^\top:|x_1|+|x_2|\leq1\},\qquad
        \lVert x\rVert_V=|x_1|+|x_2|.
    \]
    Its vertex images are
    \[
        A_1e_1=e_2,\quad A_1e_2=-e_1,\qquad
        A_2e_1=e_1,\quad A_2e_2=-e_2.
    \]
    Every image is again a vertex of the diamond.
    Thus IPA adds no vertices, and every product has operator norm at most $1$ in this norm.
    The candidate $A_2$ has spectral radius $1$, so the two inequalities give $\JSR(\mathcal{A})=1$.
    The closure is non-strict because the vertex images lie on the boundary.
\end{example}

\subsection{Correctness and Finite Termination}

The theorem proves correctness when the algorithm stops.
Finite termination requires further assumptions.
For normalized irreducible families, the strict one-generator closure characterization of \citet[Theorem 2.5]{MejstrikReif2025HybridApproachJSR} involves a spectral gap, uniqueness of the s.m.p.\ up to cyclic permutations and powers, and a simple leading eigenvalue.
That strict criterion is stronger than the non-strict invariance used in Algorithm~\ref{alg:invariantPolytope}.
Section~\ref{sec:numericsPrimaryTree} examines a candidate with two leading eigenvalues.

\section{Implementation Considerations}

\subsection{Stopping Criterion and Candidate Rejection}
\label{sec:invStoppingCriterion}

Implementations limit the vertex count and try to reject unsuitable candidates early.
Under the relevant leading-eigenvalue assumptions, \citet{GuglielmiProtasov2013ExactComputationJSR} use dual eigenvectors of cyclic candidate products for rejection tests.
Candidate rejection guides the search.
The final certificate follows from the verified polytope inclusions.

\subsection{Rebalancing and Additional Initial Vertices}
\label{sec:invRebalancing}

Balancing several candidates and adding initial vertices can improve the polytope search \citep{GuglielmiProtasov2016InvariantPolytopes}.
Additional vectors must ensure that the initial set spans the ambient space before its gauge is used as a norm.
Termination with several candidate products requires more care than in the unique-candidate setting.

\subsection{Complex Variants}
\label{sec:invEigenvectorCases}

A candidate with only nonreal leading eigenvectors does not provide a real eigenvector for the usual initialization.
Complex-balanced polytope norms are one alternative \citep{GuglielmiWirthZennaro2005ComplexPolytopeExtremality,GuglielmiZennaro2007BalancedComplexPolytopes}.
The certificates in this thesis use real matrices and real spanning vertex sets.
A real invariant polytope can still exist in the presence of nonreal eigenvalues, as Example~\ref{ex:ipaDiamondCertificate} shows.

Exact closure at the candidate scaling requires an invariant balanced polytope, which need not exist.
The tree method in the next chapter permits some unbounded internal product families while retaining a finite certificate.
